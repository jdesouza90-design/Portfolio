On September 15 the Senate failed to advance the Clarity Act, 49 to 50. American Banker called the extent to which stablecoin companies could offer yield-like products the leading issue of the debate. The day before, eight banking groups were still pushing for tighter limits on rewards, arguing they work like deposit interest.

Within three days, two Substack writers read the same failure in opposite directions. Anndy Lian headlined that stablecoin yields survive. Andrew Dresner argued the failure counts as a win for banks, because the tighter GENIUS Act restrictions now govern.

Both sides share one assumption, and it is a fair one: what a person earns for holding a stablecoin decides whether they move money into it. I agree with it. It is still the wrong screen to fight over.

The bad day is already written down

The GENIUS Act already bans issuers from paying holders any form of interest or yield in section 4(a)(11). Section 4 also requires a public redemption policy with procedures for "timely redemption". The statute never says how timely. The regulators do.

The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC) proposed its rule in March. Its section 15.12 puts timely at two business days. Then it defines the bad day. If redemption requests pass 10% of an issuer's outstanding issuance in a single 24-hour period, the window becomes seven calendar days. That applies to every request already in line and every one after it. Only the OCC can shorten it, and the OCC can also extend it further.

The rule is defensible. Regulators need some way to slow a run, and compliance is not the problem here.

The problem is what the person sees. Section 15.12 has the issuer notify the OCC within 24 hours. What it asks the issuer to tell holders is a statement in the redemption policy explaining when the period can stretch. So the one moment a stablecoin behaves least like a dollar is covered by a paragraph on a policy page.

Mechanism, labeled as transparency

The disclosures the law does require are real and useful. Once the law takes effect, an issuer publishes its outstanding supply every month, along with the composition of its reserves, down to the average tenor and where each category of reserve is held. The OCC's draft puts a link to that report in the same disclosure as the fees.

That report is for analysts. A person holding stablecoins in an app has one question on the bad day: when does my money land, and what would make it later? A reserve breakdown answers a different question, and putting it on the screen is how the industry keeps exposing its machinery and calling it transparency.

It gets harder, because a person holding a stablecoin in an exchange app may never deal with the issuer at all. As Liam Wright wrote in CryptoSlate on the day of the vote, a faster exit depends on the service converting the coin, which has to pay out before the issuer pays it. So the wait a person feels is set by whoever sits between them and the issuer. That is the same kind of platform whose rewards the Senate just fought over.

The states a balance can be in

Read the statute as a product spec and a stablecoin balance can be in four states a rewards banner never mentions. Each one needs a screen.

  • Pending. Redemption requested, due within two business days under the draft rule.
  • Extended. The 10% line was crossed by other people, and the person's own request now waits seven days.
  • Frozen. Issuers must be able to seize, freeze, burn or block transfer of coins under a lawful order, section 4(a)(6)(B).
  • In insolvency. Holders get priority on the required reserves, and section 11 asks the bankruptcy court to use best efforts to begin distributions within 14 days of the hearing.

The extended state is the one that should worry product teams. Nothing the person did caused it, and nothing on a normal balance screen would explain it.

Lending already paid for this lesson

At Best Egg, loan applicants uploaded their documents and then the dashboard went quiet. Nothing said whether a file was pending, accepted or still needed, so they called to ask. We replaced it with a status for every document, sized at 7,000 fewer support calls a month.

Stablecoin redemption is that screen with the person's own money behind it. A silent "processing" on day four of a seven-day extension does not read as a rule working as designed. It reads as a freeze. Jayson Derrick wrote about the trigger in May and pictured retail holders opening their apps on a Sunday night to find "a locked gate." That is the screen to draw first.

The screen I ask for first

Put a stablecoin product in front of me and the first screen I would ask for is the balance on day four of an extended redemption. What does it say happened, what does it say happens next and on what date?

If the team shows me the rewards rate instead, the product has been designed for the day money arrives and not for the day it tries to leave.

Comptroller Jonathan Gould said in August that the OCC wants a final rule out by November. Under section 20 the law takes effect no later than January 18, 2027. The rewards fight, American Banker reports, starts over in the next Congress. The redemption screen gets built before then either way, and almost nobody in this month's fight is talking about it.
