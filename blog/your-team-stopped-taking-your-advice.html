Design leadership

Your team stopped taking your advice

In one year, the number of designers learning from each other went from a quarter to more than two thirds. Over the same year, the number taking recommendations from their leadership fell by half. Both of those are the same event.

Published on 22 September 2026 · 4 min read

You are no longer the fastest source of useful knowledge on your team, and trying to win that back is the most expensive mistake available to you right now.

Here are two numbers from a survey of more than 900 designers. Peer learning went from 24% to 70% in a year. The share of designers taking recommendations from their leadership went from 32% to 16%.

The first number gets quoted constantly, usually as a feel-good line about community. The second one barely gets quoted at all, and it is the one that should be keeping design leaders up.

The comfortable reading is wrong

The comfortable reading is that this is a culture problem. Teams got scattered, people stopped looking up, leaders need to communicate better and be more present.

I do not think that is what happened. I think the method started turning over faster than any management chain can carry it.

The average designer’s toolstack went from three tools to seven in 12 months. When the useful knowledge has a shelf life measured in weeks, the person three levels up is structurally the last to hear it. Not because they are disengaged. Because that is what a hierarchy does to information that expires: it delays it.

Your team did not lose respect for you. They found a faster path, and they were right to take it.

The expensive mistake

The expensive mistake is trying to win method authority back.

It looks reasonable from the inside. You block time, you learn the tools, you show up to the design review with a working prototype you built yourself. And it does buy you something real, which is that you stop being illegible to your own team.

But you cannot win that race. There are eight of them and one of you, they are in the tools all day, and whatever you learn this quarter is half wrong by the next. Meanwhile the thing only you can do is sitting there not getting done.

I watch leaders spend their scarce hours becoming the fourth-best prototyper on a team of six. It is the most sympathetic failure mode I know and it is still a failure mode.

Make the trade explicit

The trade I have settled on is that the team owns the method and I own the standard and the decision.

Owning the standard means I am accountable for writing down what good means here, precisely enough that someone who was not in the room can hold work to it. Not a mood, not a principle poster. The actual bar, in words, that a person or a model can be measured against.

Owning the decision means the calls that need a name on them stay with me. Which of two entrenched options we standardize on. What we deprecate when the data is genuinely tied. Anything where somebody’s work is on the other end of the answer.

When I audited our design library this year, the analysis came back saying one spacing value appeared 1,834 times and the competing one 1,796. Nothing in the data was ever going to break that tie. Somebody had to pick, say so out loud, and then go make the other one wrong. That is not a job you can delegate downward or automate, and it is the job.

What I do not do any more is pretend I know the best way to get from a brief to a prototype this month. They know. I ask.

What this looks like on a calendar

Three things I actually changed, in case the principle is too tidy to be useful.

  • I ask what changed in your method, every one-on-one. Not as a check-in, as intake. It is how I find out what the team knows before the quarter is over. Half of what I now believe about working with agents came off that question.
  • Whoever found the better way presents it, not me. The moment a leader relays somebody else’s discovery, two things break: the credit and the accuracy.
  • I write the standard down before anyone touches a tool. If the team cannot say in a paragraph what a correct component looks like, no amount of tooling discovers it. It averages whatever is already there and hands it back with confidence.

The part almost nobody has done

The same survey found that only 28% of leaders made any formal change to evaluation, pay or hiring. Just 8% changed performance metrics and 4% touched compensation.

So the work changed, who people learn from changed, and what we reward stayed exactly where it was. That gap is where good designers quietly decide their leadership is not paying attention, and it is a gap leaders can close without permission from anyone.

Start with the review template. If it still rewards volume of screens, it is measuring the part of the job that got cheap.

There is one number in that report I find genuinely reassuring: 80% of designers still trust their own quality judgment over the model’s. Taste did not go anywhere. What went is the assumption that it flows downhill from the person with the biggest title.

Sources

  1. AI in Design 2026, Designer Fund and Foundation Capital. More than 900 designers across 60 countries, 25 interviews. 28% of leaders made formal updates to evaluation, pay or hiring, with 8% changing performance metrics and 4% compensation.
  2. Teams, the same report, on how designers learn: peer learning 24% to 70%, leadership recommendations 32% to 16%.
  3. Craft, the same report, on what designers still judge for themselves: 80% rely on their own quality judgment over the model's.
