# Position

What John argues on the blog, and what he won't. Read this with [VOICE.md](VOICE.md)
before drafting a post: VOICE.md is how the site writes, this is what it believes.

## What this blog is for

A hiring manager reads the case studies to see what John shipped. They read the blog to
see **how he thinks and how he operates** before they ever get him on a call.

So a post takes a position on a conversation the industry is actually having right now.
It is not a case study retold. John's own work appears as **evidence in a sentence or
two**, never as the subject. If a draft starts to read like "here is a project I did and
what I learned", it has failed, and the fix is to find the argument it was avoiding.

Three pillars. A post belongs to exactly one. A post that fits none of them doesn't get
written.

## Pillar 1 — Design leadership (`leadership`)

How John runs a team, hires, evaluates and decides. The live conversation: leaders have
lost their position as the fastest source of useful knowledge, and almost none of them
have changed how they lead in response.

**The evidence.** Peer learning among designers went from 24% to 80% in a year, while the
share taking recommendations from leadership fell from 32% to 16%. Only 28% of leaders
made any formal change to evaluation, 8% changed performance metrics, 4% touched
compensation (AI in Design 2026, 900 designers across 60 countries).

**John's position.** That is not a discipline problem, it is a structural one: when the
method turns over every quarter, the person three levels up is the last to know. The trade
is that the team owns the method and the leader owns the standard and the decision. A
leader who tries to win back method authority loses both.

**What he does not argue.** That leaders should just "be learners too". That flat teams
solve it. That any of this is a reason to manage less.

## Pillar 2 — Where product design is heading (`craft`)

What the job becomes. The live conversation: the role is expanding and thinning at the
same time, and hiring has not caught up with either.

**The evidence.** 91% of designers use AI weekly and 75% daily. Half have shipped
AI-generated code to production. 65% report taking on more product or engineering work and
40% say product managers and engineers now contribute more to design. 73% feel rising
expectations for output, quality and speed. The average toolstack went from three tools to
seven in one year, and 50% of leaders now screen candidates for AI fluency.

**John's position.** Screening for AI fluency is screening for recency. The scarce skill
is the ability to write down what good means precisely enough that someone who was not in
the room can hold work to it, and then to say why the model's answer was rejected. That
was always the job. It is only now that not having it is visible.

**What he does not argue.** That AI replaces designers. That prompting is a craft. That
every designer must ship code.

## Pillar 3 — Product management (`product`)

Where product management and product design meet: how the two roles divide the work,
decide together, and change as AI moves more of the build into both. Product strategy,
discovery, prioritization and the PM and design partnership, argued from the design
leader's side of the table.

**The evidence.** 65% of designers report taking on more product or engineering work,
and 40% say product managers and engineers now contribute more to design (AI in Design
2026, 900 designers across 60 countries).

**John's position.** Not yet written. Until John adds it here, a post in this pillar
argues from the positions in pillars 1 and 2 as they apply to the product and design
boundary, and states nothing as John's view that this file, VOICE.md or an existing
post does not already say.

**What he does not argue.** That designers should become PMs, or PMs designers.

## Out of scope

Fintech, payments, banking, open banking, lending, stablecoins, crypto and web3 are not
blog topics, even with a design angle. John's work in those industries can appear as
evidence in a sentence or two, per rule 3, never as the subject of a post.

## Rules for every post

1. **Start from the conversation, not from John.** Name the position the industry holds,
   fairly, in one sentence. Then disagree with something specific about it. A post that
   agrees with everyone is a press release.
2. **One claim, carried by the title.** A reader who stops after the title has the
   position.
3. **Experience is evidence, not subject.** A sentence or two, doing a job in the
   argument. If a paragraph is narrating a project, cut it.
4. **Every number names and links its source.** Never invent, round or extrapolate. If the
   number cannot be found, the sentence loses the number, not the source.
5. **Show how John operates.** At least one concrete thing he actually does: the question
   he asks in an interview, the rule he holds, the call he makes. A hiring manager should
   finish the post knowing something about working with him.
6. **Credit precisely.** "I" for what John decided, "we" for what the team shipped. Team
   sizes are the project's designers, not the org. Never claim a colleague's build.
7. **No predictions without a source.** "Sized", "opportunity" and "so far" are available.
   Forecasts are not.
8. **800 to 1,200 words.**

## Attribution, precisely

The button component audit at Best Egg was one senior product designer's from start to
finish, agent and remediation. John built and ran the whole-library audit, wrote the
crawler's rules and directed the remediation. Never blur the two.

## The test

Would John say this out loud to a room of design leaders who disagreed with him, and have
the receipts ready? If not, it does not go up.
